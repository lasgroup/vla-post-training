Abstract

The introduction of generalist robot policies has enabled a single, large model to learn a variety of tasks from a vast pre-training corpus. While these models often display powerful zero-shot generalization capabilities, they nonetheless typically require an adaptation phase to reach sufficient performance in deployment. Iterated self-improvement via reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated promise in enabling this adaptation, yet such approaches have been largely explored in single-task settings—where the goal is to simply improve on a narrow deployment task—which lies in stark contrast to the multi-task capabilities of the base policy.

In this work we ask whether generalist robot policies are capable of multi-task, generalist improvement in deployment—targeting many diverse tasks simultaneously—and what algorithmic recipe enables this. To answer this question, we first develop a set of structured evaluations to test generalist improvement capabilities, and systematically evaluate algorithmic and practical design choices regarding value estimation, policy extraction and resource allocation.

We show that not only generalist policies can be improved online across diverse tasks, but that the right recipe can enable generalization and increase performance on tasks not considered during online training. We characterize when this generalization occurs, and show that the training task distribution as well as the use of value-based RL are both critical. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first evidence for online generalist improvement for robot foundation models.

Motivation

Can a generalist robot become a better generalist?

Online post-training often improves a robot on one deployment task while degrading its performance elsewhere. We ask whether experience across many tasks can improve the whole policy: both on the tasks it practices and on related tasks it has never encountered during post-training.

Method

Two ways to learn from a robot’s own experience

Starting from π0.5, we isolate policy adaptation from critic-guided action selection. Both operate in an iterative loop of online data collection and learning.

Direct policy adaptation

Filtered supervised fine-tuning

FSFT

Collect rollouts↓Keep successful trajectories↓Update the full VLA

Fine-tune the policy on its successful experience using a supervised flow-matching objective.

Learns by updating the policy parameters.

Critic-guided refinement

Best-of-N action selection

BoN

Sample 32 action chunks↓Score candidates with a learned critic↓Execute the highest-value candidate

Train a value function with temporal-difference learning and use it to rank actions from the frozen base policy.

Learns a critic; keeps the policy parameters fixed.

Shared protocol: 10 improvement iterations · 20 episodes per task per iteration · 1–64 training tasks

What kind of diversity matters?

Transfer follows the training distribution

Diversifying scenes helps transfer to new scenes; diversifying objects helps transfer to new objects. Only scaling object diversity surpasses the base policy on both axes in this comparison.

Generalization depends on the kinds of behaviors a robot practices, as well as how many tasks it sees.

What makes the recipe work?

Simple choices have a large effect

Adapt the full policy

Full VLA tuning improves FSFT on training tasks and unseen scenes, but increases the risk of forgetting on unseen objects.

Use a residual critic

BroNet residual connections improve performance by more than 10 percentage points over a standard MLP critic.

Learn with TD regression

Temporal-difference targets with a regression loss give the largest and most consistent gains in the critic ablations.

Combining policy and value learning

Filtering is a key ingredient

FSFT+BoN can bring modest gains on training tasks, but does not improve generalization beyond the standalone methods. In the LIBERO comparisons, filtering successful trajectories is crucial for stable policy updates.

Adding FSFT-style filtering to PARL closes much of the gap to FSFT+BoN.

Highlights

01Multi-task improvement is possible. A single generalist policy can improve across a diverse collection of training tasks.

02Value learning enables transfer. With sufficient task diversity, critic-guided refinement transitions from forgetting to generalization.

03The task distribution matters. Diverse manipulation behaviors are essential for transferring to new objects.

Scope and limitations

These are empirical findings in simulated manipulation environments. Tasks were selected so the base policy could make non-trivial progress. Generalization is strongest in the structured MolmoSpaces setting; LIBERO remains harder. Transfer to real hardware and whole-body control is an open direction.
